Abstract

Tournament mortality of Largemouth Bass Micropterus salmoides can be a major source of population mortality. Tournament captures and mortality may be reduced through restrictive harvest regulations (bag and length limits), but these approaches for mitigating tournament mortality have not been assessed. We assessed the effects of bass tournament angler bag and minimum length limits on the number of bass weighed in, bass tournament weigh‐in probability, and the reduction in tournament mortality. We tagged nearly 4,000 Largemouth Bass during 205 tournaments and electrofishing events at Brushy Creek Lake, Iowa, from 2015 to 2019. We then used a before–after–control–impact analysis to evaluate the effect of tournament regulations (bag limit: either three or five bass; minimum length limit: no minimum or 305, 330, 356, or 381 mm) on bass monthly catch rates and a capture–recapture model to assess tournament weigh‐in probability among regulation types. Number of bass per tournament angler‐hour was similar among a three‐bass, ≥381‐mm length limit and more liberalized regulations (mean = 0.26 bass/angler/h). Length and bag limits had little effect on bass weigh‐in probability. We estimated that reducing the bag limit from five to three bass would have little effect, while a reduction from three to two bass would reduce the number of bass weighed in by 35% and a bag limit of one bass would result in a 62% reduction. Alternatively, a minimum length limit of ≥381 mm could reduce the number of bass weighed in by over 40%. Tournament mortality was greatest in July and August, but low catch rates during these months resulted in little mortality. Although current length and bag limits did not affect bass weigh‐in probability, the number of bass weighed in and associated tournament mortality may be reduced if bag and length limits are very restrictive (≤2 bass/angler or minimum length ≥381 mm). However, restrictive tournament regulations may not be biologically necessary or socially acceptable.

Black bass Micropterus spp. constitute a highly studied genus and provide many recreational angling opportunities. Due to their adaptability and tolerance to a wide range of habitats, black bass have risen in popularity with anglers since the 1970s (Hayes et al. 1995; Long et al. 2015; USFWS 2016). As the popularity of black bass recreational angling increased, the popularity of competitive live‐release tournaments likewise increased (Schramm et al. 1991). However, despite the current live‐release format, black bass tournaments may increase mortality related to catch‐and‐release angling, potentially adversely affecting black bass populations (Wilde 1998; Suski et al. 2003; Allen et al. 2004; Hysmith et al. 2014).

Most black bass tournaments in the United States today are live‐release events in which all captured individuals are returned to the water (Long et al. 2015; Schramm and Gilliland 2015; Cooke et al. 2020). However, not all fish survive the capture, confinement, and weigh‐in process (Kwak and Henry 1995; Wilde 1998; Long et al. 2015; Schramm and Gilliland 2015; Sylvia and Weber 2019; Maahs et al. 2021). Stress associated with prolonged live‐well confinement, exposure to poor water quality, and handling has led to total tournament mortality ranging from 5% to 68% of captured individuals (Hayes et al. 1995; Kwak and Henry 1995; Driscoll et al. 2007; Sylvia and Weber 2019; Maahs et al. 2021). Additionally, bass tournament anglers are efficient at locating and capturing bass (Detmer et al. 2020; Sylvia et al. 2020) and can capture a large percentage (5–43%) of some Largemouth Bass M. salmoides populations over the course of a season (Driscoll et al. 2007; Hysmith et al. 2014; Sylvia and Weber 2019). Furthermore, catch‐and‐release mortality is the largest source of angling mortality for many bass populations (Allen et al. 2004; Hysmith et al. 2014; Sylvia and Weber 2019; Sylvia et al. 2021a, 2021b). Therefore, tournament fishing may be negatively affecting bass populations through tournament‐induced mortality (Hayes et al. 1995; Allen et al. 1998, 2004; Wilde 1998; Sylvia et al. 2021b).

Harvest regulations, such as length and bag limits, are used to limit the size and number of fish that anglers can harvest and to prevent overfishing in highly exploited populations (Noble and Jones 1999; Isermann and Paukert 2010). Similarly, harvest regulations could be a tool for reducing the number of bass weighed in at fishing tournaments and subsequently reducing tournament mortality. Throughout the United States, black bass bag and minimum length tournament regulations are highly variable (e.g., ADCNR 2020; Florida FWC 2020; Iowa DNR 2020; Louisiana DNR 2020; Texas Parks and Wildlife Department 2020). The effectiveness of length and bag limits for managing the harvest of recreational fisheries dominated by live‐release angling is variable (Wilde 1997) but often low (Cook et al. 2001; Sylvia et al. 2021a) due to the majority of anglers harvesting fewer fish than allowed (Radomski et al. 2001; Isermann et al. 2007; Mosel et al. 2015; Moreau and Matthias 2018). However, it is unclear whether bag and length limits can reduce the number of bass weighed in and subsequent tournament mortality due to high catch rates by skilled tournament anglers (Fisher 1997; Sylvia et al. 2020) or whether these limits provide no benefits due to tournament anglers currently weighing in fewer fish than allowed by existing regulations.

Beyond bag and size limit regulations, a suite of different factors likely influences black bass capture probability, which determines the number of bass that anglers are able to capture and weigh in at tournaments. Bass captures by tournament anglers vary seasonally in relation to environmental conditions (Sylvia et al. 2020), as increased catch rates occur due to increased metabolism with warming water temperature leading into the spawning period (Suski et al. 2003; Suski and Philipp 2004). Tournament angler effort may increase the potential overlap between black bass and anglers, thus increasing capture probability (Sylvia et al. 2020), while total tournament catch may account for differences among tournaments that influence black bass capture unaccounted for by other factors. However, the relative effects of length and bag limits compared to environmental conditions and tournament angler effort on the probability of Largemouth Bass (hereafter, referred to as “bass”) being captured and weighed in during a live‐release tournament are unknown.

Our objectives were to (1) assess whether tournament anglers weighed in more fish under liberalized limits compared to a ≥381‐mm minimum length limit with a three‐fish bag limit; (2) evaluate the relative importance of bass tournament bag and minimum length limit regulations, environmental characteristics (i.e., water temperature and wind speed), and tournament angler effort for influencing bass tournament weigh‐in probability; and (3) estimate potential reductions in the number of bass weighed in and tournament mortality under a range of reduced bag limits and increased minimum length limits. We hypothesized that anglers in tournaments with a lower minimum length limit and a five‐bass bag limit would weigh in more bass, whereas more conservative minimum length and bag limits would reduce the bass weigh‐in probability. We also hypothesized that a bag limit of less than three bass and a length limit greater than 381 mm would be needed before the number of bass weighed in and the associated tournament mortality could be reduced.

METHODS

Study Site

Approximately 40 live‐release bass tournaments are held per year (mean = 23.92 tournament angler‐hours/ha/year from 2015 to 2019) during the open‐water season (April–October) at Brushy Creek Lake, Iowa (279 ha). From 1987 through 2017, bass tournament anglers in Iowa could weigh in up to three black bass at a lake‐specific minimum length limit, typically ≥381 mm (≥15 in). Under these regulations, up to 30% of the black bass population was weighed in annually at tournaments held at Brushy Creek Lake (Sylvia and Weber 2019; Sylvia et al. 2021b). In 2017, the Iowa legislature passed new regulations allowing black bass tournament anglers to weigh in up to five bass with no minimum length limit, starting in 2018. Bass tournaments during 2018–2019 at Brushy Creek Lake adopted a variety of self‐imposed bag limits (either a three‐ or five‐bass limit) and minimum length limits (no minimum or 305, 330, 356, or 381 mm), providing an opportunity to assess how variation in tournament regulations may affect bass tournament weigh‐in probability relative to environmental conditions (i.e., wind speed and water temperature) and angler effort (i.e., total tournament angling hours or the number of bass weighed in).

Sampling

Electrofishing

We conducted monthly boat electrofishing (pulsed DC, 300 V, 12A) across the entire accessible shoreline of Brushy Creek Lake (~3 d/month; mean monthly electrofishing duration ±95% CI = 8.3 ± 4.9 h) from April to November in 2018 and 2019. All untagged bass larger than 330 mm were weighed (g) and measured (mm), were tagged on the upper left jaw with an individually numbered metal Monel butt‐end band, and received a year‐specific fin clip to enumerate tag loss. We selected Monel tags for their high retention in black bass but noted the initial tagging year for tag loss based on year‐specific fin clips. Recaptured bass were measured (mm) and weighed (g), and the tag number was recorded before the fish were released.

Bass tournaments

We censused all bass that were weighed in during tournaments at Brushy Creek Lake from 2015 to 2019. We recorded the self‐imposed tournament regulations (minimum length limit and bag limit) for each tournament, along with the number of anglers, tournament duration (h), number of bass weighed in, initial bass mortalities (dead fish at tournament weigh‐in), mean water temperature (HOBO Pendant Temperature/Light 64K Data Logger, 15‐min sampling intervals; Onset Computer Corporation, Bourne, Massachusetts) from four locations within the lake at 0.0 and 4.6 m, and mean air temperature (°C) and wind speed (km/h; Weather Underground, https://www.wunderground.com/history). Weigh‐in procedures were not standardized among tournaments; however, all tournaments transported bass from anglers' boats to the weigh‐in table in water‐filled bags. After tournament weigh‐in, we placed bass in water‐filled tanks with supplemental oxygen. We examined each bass for a jaw tag or clipped fins and we recorded the tag number of recaptured bass along with weight (g) and lengths were measured as TL (mm). Similar to electroshocked bass, all untagged bass were given a metal Monel tag on the top left jaw and a year‐specific fin clip to enumerate tag loss. Once processed, all bass were released from the tournament weigh‐in location.

Radiotelemetry

We implanted radiotelemetry tags (F1835; 14 g in water; 897‐d battery life; Advanced Telemetry Systems [ATS], Isanti, Minnesota) in 65 bass during April 2018 and an additional 29 bass in April 2019. See Maahs et al. (2021) for additional telemetry tagging details. We then tracked bass weekly by boat from April to November in 2018 and 2019 by using an ATS R4000 telemetry radio receiver and a three‐element folding Yagi antenna. Bass locations were determined when the receiver gain was at the lowest achievable setting and the signal was unidirectional. Range testing and visual confirmations indicated that bass were within 2 m. We considered a telemetry bass to be dead if it was detected in the same location for five consecutive weeks, whereas we considered it to be alive if it was found in different locations and was visually sighted periodically.

Recreational anglers

Recreational anglers reported recaptured bass through the tag reporting phone number or e‐mail address displayed on signs around the lake. While conducting field work, personal communication with anglers also provided recapture data. Anglers were asked to report the tag number, capture date, capture location, and whether the bass was released or harvested.

Analysis

Before–after–control–impact model

We used a before–after–control–impact (BACI) model (Stewart‐Oaten and Bence 2001) based on tournament catch during May–September to compare the number of bass per tournament angler‐hour for the years before (2015–2017) and after (2018–2019) the tournament regulation change. Although tournaments did occur during April and October (mean = 2.5 tournaments/month), we removed those months from the BACI analysis because there were not enough tournaments in each month to fit the group × time interaction. We assigned each tournament to one of two groups: tournament events that used a three‐fish, ≥381‐mm limit or tournament events that adopted a five‐fish bag and a minimum length limit less than 381 mm. Events using a three‐bass, ≥381‐mm limit represented tournaments that followed the tournament regulations used prior to the legislative change in 2018. After the regulation change, most tournament groups adopted new regulations. However, one local club that conducted weekly tournaments maintained the previous regulations (three‐fish, ≥381‐mm limit) and served as the control group in the BACI analysis to separate changes in bass catch as a result of regulation changes compared to temporal variation. We used an ordinary least‐squares linear model in program R (R Core Team 2014) to fit the fixed effects of group, time, and year along with a group × time interaction. A significant group × time interaction between pre‐ and postregulation change years indicated an effect of the regulation (Stewart‐Oaten and Bence 2001). Using our linear model estimates, we created a contrast for each month to estimate differences between groups and time periods. To account for temporal and between‐group variability, group × time interaction contrasts were estimated as

impact/after−control/before−control/after–impact/before.

Contrast estimates and associated 95% CIs were used to determine whether tournament groups that adopted new tournament regulations weighed in more bass per tournament angler‐hour than tournaments that used a three‐bass, ≥381‐mm limit. Any 95% CI that did not encompass zero was considered significant.

Multistate model

To evaluate the effect of length and bag limits on bass weigh‐in probability during live‐release tournaments, we analyzed daily individual bass encounter histories from capture–recapture data (electrofishing, tournaments, telemetry, or recreational angler reports) during the 2018 and 2019 tournament seasons using a live‐capture multistate model in program MARK to obtain maximum likelihood estimates of survival (S), detection probability (P), and bass weigh‐in probability (transition of bass from Brushy Creek Lake to a tournament [Ψ]; White et al. 2006). Details of multistate model frameworks, their assumptions, and their use in fisheries research can be found in Sylvia et al. (2021b) and Sylvia and Weber (2022). In our specific model, bass could reside in one of three states (Figure S1 available in the Supplementary Materials in the online version of this article). A bass was considered to be in Brushy Creek Lake (B in Figure S1) until captured by a tournament angler and weighed in during a tournament, at which point it transitioned into the tournament state (T in Figure S1). Thus, our estimate of weigh‐in probability (referred to as “capture probability” in Sylvia et al. 2021a; Sylvia and Weber 2022) only included bass that were weighed in at tournaments and not individuals that were released prior to the weigh‐in (e.g., culled bass). As the vast majority of anglers did not weigh in their limit, instances of culling were likely very low throughout the study.

To reduce the number of parameters to estimate, we fixed known transition and survival rates when possible (for examples, see Sylvia et al. 2020, 2021a; Sylvia and Weber 2022). Parameters are difficult to estimate in multistate models with more than one unobservable state. Therefore, we fixed tournament survival to estimates previously derived from this data set using a Cormack–Jolly–Seber model of 3‐d postrelease tournament survival (Maahs et al. 2021). A full description of our multistate model framework can be found in Supplementary Materials S1 (available in the online version of the article).

We used time‐varying covariates to evaluate factors affecting bass detection probability, survival, and tournament weigh‐in probability at Brushy Creek Lake. Covariates that were used to describe variability in detection probabilities for jaw‐tagged and radiotelemetry‐tagged bass included tag type group (jaw or telemetry), electrofishing effort (h), water temperature (°C), and quadratic effects of water temperature and air temperature (°C). Water temperature and the quadratic effect of water temperature affect bass survival (Sylvia and Weber 2019) and were the only covariates included for survival estimates within Brushy Creek Lake. To meet our objective of identifying the effects of tournament regulations on weigh‐in probability, tournament bag and length limit covariates were compared against models that incorporated covariates representing angler and environmental factors. Covariates representing angler factors on weigh‐in probabilities were tournament angler effort (number of anglers participating multiplied by tournament duration [h]) and the total number of bass weighed in at tournaments. Environmental covariates were wind speed (km/h), the quadratic effect of wind speed, water temperature, and air temperature.

We identified supported covariates based on a hierarchical modeling process in which we used Akaike's information criterion with the small sample size correction (AICc) and AICc weights to identify the most parsimonious models. We developed models to describe variation in detection probability (P) as the first step in the hierarchical modeling process; we then developed models of survival in Brushy Creek Lake and finally the probability of bass weigh‐in by tournament anglers. We first fixed survival in Brushy Creek Lake and the transition from Brushy Creek Lake to a tournament at an intercept to identify important covariates on detection probability in Brushy Creek Lake. With few telemetry bass captured during electrofishing events across 2 years (n = 7), electrofishing effort was only included for the jaw‐tagged group. Using the most supported detection probability structure, water temperature and the quadratic effect of water temperature were included in the model of bass survival in Brushy Creek Lake. Continuing to build upon the most supported model structure, we tested covariate combinations on the transition from Brushy Creek Lake to a tournament to identify factors that influenced weigh‐in probability. Total tournament catch was a better predictor of weigh‐in probability than total angler effort (AIC difference [∆AICc] = 23.33), indicating that other factors beyond angler effort determined the weigh‐in probability for an individual bass. We only included this effect in our top model to avoid correlated covariates (r = 0.63) and to simplify our models. Similarly, both water temperature and air temperature were used but not within the same model (r = 0.83). We compared models containing tournament bag and length regulations to models with angler factors (tournament effort and total tournament catch) and environmental factors (wind speed, water temperature, and air temperature). Our intent for the multistate model was to estimate bass weigh‐in probability at tournaments and subsequent tournament mortality. Thus, we only present weigh‐in probability results, but we include bass detection probability and survival estimates in Supplementary Materials S1.

Length and bag limit simulations

We used 2018 and 2019 tournament weigh‐in census data from Brushy Creek Lake to estimate the effects of a reduced bag limit on all observed tournament regulations. For each tournament, we estimated the reduction in the number of bass that would have been weighed in if the bag limit had been decreased to four, three, two, or one bass per tournament angler. Similarly, using tournaments with a minimum length limit <381 mm, we estimated the reduction in the number of bass that would have been weighed in if the minimum length limit had been between the smallest (270‐mm) and largest (570‐mm) observed bass that were weighed in. The overall percent reduction in tournament weigh‐in achieved under a reduced bag limit or increased minimum length limit was calculated as the number of bass that would have been released under a four‐, three‐, two‐, or one‐bass bag limit or increased minimum length limits divided by the number of observed bass weighed in at each tournament (% reduction = number of bass released/number of bass observed; Isermann et al. 2007).

Reducing the number of bass weighed in at live‐release tournaments reduces the number of bass that are susceptible to tournament mortality. Therefore, we estimated the number of tournament mortalities for each simulated reduced bag limit and minimum length limit scenario. We multiplied the cumulative 3‐d mortality (1 – survival) estimate (Maahs et al. 2021) by the number of bass that would have been weighed in under a four‐, three‐, two‐, or one‐bass bag limit or increased minimum length limits. To estimate the percent reduction in mortality using different regulations, we compared these mortality estimates to estimates based on actual angler practices observed during 2018 and 2019.

RESULTS

Between 39 and 42 tournaments were held annually for a total of 205 tournaments censused between 2015 and 2019 (Table 1). During 2015–2017, all tournaments (n = 125) followed a three‐fish, ≥381‐mm limit, whereas 59% (n = 47) of the total tournaments followed this regulation during 2018–2019. The remaining tournaments during 2018–2019 (n = 33) used a combination of three‐ and five‐fish bag limits with minimum length limits ranging from none to ≥381 mm (Table 2). Across all tournaments during 2018–2019, 43% of anglers weighed in zero bass, 14% of anglers weighed in one bass, 11% of anglers weighed in two bass, 21% of anglers weighed in three bass, 2% of anglers weighed in four bass, and 9% of anglers weighed in five bass. The number of bass weighed in per angler per tournament hour from 2015 to 2019 ranged from 0.03 bass in August 2017 to 0.67 bass in May 2018. Groups using a five‐fish bag limit with a ≥356‐mm limit in 2018 (0.45 bass per angler per hour) and 2019 (0.44 bass per angler per hour) had the highest number of bass per angler per tournament hour (Table 2).

Length of bass at weigh‐in also varied with tournament regulations in 2018 and 2019. Of the bass weighed in by tournaments with a ≥305‐, ≥330‐, or ≥356‐mm limit or no minimum length limit (new regulations), 20.1% in 2018 and 16.6% in 2019 were smaller than 381 mm (Figure 1). The highest proportion (63.2%) of bass less than 381 mm weighed in at tournaments under a given regulation occurred during the one tournament that used a five‐bass bag limit with no minimum length limit, followed by tournaments using a ≥305‐mm (51.6%), ≥330‐mm (37.3%), or ≥356‐mm (28.7%) limit (Figure 2). Conversely, the proportion of 457‐mm and larger bass in 2018 was higher when tournaments used a three‐bass limit with a ≥381‐mm length limit (20.5%) compared to a five‐bass limit with smaller (≥305‐, ≥330‐, or ≥356‐mm) length limits (16.2%; Figure 1). The proportion of 457‐mm and larger bass in 2019 was similar between tournaments with a three‐bass limit and 381‐mm length limit (10.7%) and tournaments with a combination of smaller length limits (≥305, ≥330, or ≥356 mm) and a bag limit of five bass (10.4%; Figure 1).

Before–After–Control–Impact Model

The number of tournaments during the preregulation change years (2015–2017) included 70 control tournaments and 55 impact tournaments. In the years after the regulation change (2018 and 2019), there were 47 control tournaments and 33 impact tournaments. Average number of bass/angler/h varied among tournament group types and years, with the maximum occurring in May 2018 for groups with a three‐bass, ≥381‐mm regulation (0.55 bass/angler/h; 95% CI = 0.0, 1.1) and in June 2018 for groups adopting new regulations (0.44 bass/angler/h; 95% CI = −0.1, 1.0; Figure 3B). We found no interactive effect of group × time (0.01; SE = 0.03; t = 0.42; P = 0.68), suggesting that tournaments did not weigh in more bass/angler/h after increasing the tournament bag limit to five bass and removing the minimum length limit in 2018 (Figure 4B). Next, we analyzed each year separately to test for different responses in catch rates within each month during 2018 and 2019 and found a significant group × time effect in June 2018 (0.17; t = 2.26, P = 0.03), with the CI not including zero (95% CI = 0.02, 0.33); tournament angler groups that adopted new regulations caught significantly more bass/angler/h than groups using the former regulations in June 2018 (Figure 4A). However, we detected no other differences between angler groups during 2018 or 2019 (P > 0.05).

Multistate Model

We jaw‐tagged 2,515 bass (≥330 mm) at 80 tournaments and 1,356 bass during 131 h of electrofishing, and an additional 93 bass were tagged with radiotelemetry transmitters (Table 3). Three models received support in describing bass weigh‐in probability using tournament regulations, environmental factors, and tournament angler covariates (Table 4). The most supported model (Table 4) indicated that bass weigh‐in probability was positively related to the number of bass weighed in at a tournament (β = 0.0184; 95% CI = 0.0128, 0.0241) and to water temperature (β = 0.0451; 95% CI = 0.0058, 0.0844; Figure 5), whereas bass weigh‐in probability increased with wind speed to 12.2 km/h and then declined as wind speed exceeded 12.2 km/h (wind speed: β = 0.1654; 95% CI = 0.0269, 0.3038; wind speed2: β = −0.0067; 95% CI = −0.0124, −0.0011; Figure 5). With a constant mean wind speed (11.3 km/h), bass weigh‐in probability ranged from 0.0342 (95% CI = 0.0002, 0.8880) at a maximum water temperature of 28.72°C and a maximum of 140 bass weighed in to 0.0010 (95% CI = <0.0001, 0.0411) at a minimum water temperature of 5.71°C and a minimum of four bass weighed in (Figure 5A). When the number of bass weighed in was 41 bass/tournament (mean across tournaments), the maximum bass weigh‐in probability was estimated at 0.0057 (95% CI = <0.0001, 0.4838) when wind speed was 12.2 km/h and water temperature was 28.79°C (Figure 5B). The effects of bag limits (β = −0.0152; 95% CI = −0.2085, 0.1781) and length limits (β = 0.1876; 95% CI = −0.0916, 0.4657) were included in the second most supported model. An additional quadratic effect of water temperature (water temperature: β = 0.1074; 95% CI = −0.1976, 0.4124; water temperature2: β = −0.0015; 95% CI = −0.0089, 0.0058) was included as a covariate used to describe bass weigh‐in probability in our third most supported model (Table 4). However, the effects of length limit, bag limit, and the quadratic effect of water temperature in our second and third most supported models had estimates with 95% CIs that encompassed zero. Bass survival and detection probability results are presented in Supplementary Materials S1.

In 2018, reducing the bag limit from five to four bass resulted in an estimated 11 fewer bass dying from total tournament mortality (a 5.6% reduction), followed by an estimated 14 fewer bass with a three‐fish bag limit (12.4% reduction), 42 fewer bass with a two‐fish bag limit (33.3% reduction), and 55 fewer bass with a one‐fish bag limit (60.6% reduction; Figure 9B). Under observed angler practices in July, percent tournament mortality was predicted to be 14% in 2018 and 15% in 2019, but mortality was predicted to decrease to 7% in 2018 and to 6% in 2019 under a simulated one‐bass bag limit (Figure 7). DISCUSSION

Bag and length limits did not affect the number of bass weighed in by tournament anglers at Brushy Creek Lake, as over 40% of anglers weighed in no bass and overall bass capture rates were low. In recreational fisheries, low catchability also reduces an angler's ability to fill bag limits, resulting in minimal effects of harvest regulations on a population in many instances (Wilde 1997; Noble and Jones 1999; Radomski et al. 2001). Instead, our results suggest that environmental characteristics were more important for describing variation in bass weigh‐in probability than length and bag limit regulations. However, our results also indicate that the number of bass weighed in could be reduced if regulations were more conservative than those used by the tournaments in this study (e.g., bag limits ≤2 bass or minimum length limits >381 mm). While our results suggest that more conservative harvest regulations are not necessary on Brushy Creek Lake, harvest regulations could be effective tools for reducing the number of bass that are exposed to tournament stressors and for reducing subsequent tournament mortality for other populations in which bass tournament captures and mortality are higher. However, potential biological benefits of more conservative regulations along with potential associated social ramifications must be considered before changes in tournament regulations are enacted (Schramm and Hunt 2007).

Bass tournament anglers are highly skilled at locating and capturing bass (Fisher 1997; Sylvia et al. 2020) and can weigh in large portions of bass populations (Wilde 1998; Hysmith et al. 2014). However, we found that anglers only weighed in an average (±95% CI) of 0.26 ± 0.04 bass per angler per tournament hour, over 40% of tournament anglers did not weigh in a bass, and length and bag limits did not affect the number of bass weighed in by tournament anglers. The angler catch rates that we observed at Brushy Creek Lake are similar to those reported for tournaments in other states, including Alabama (0.35 bass per angler per tournament hour; Bolton 2016), California (0.33 bass per angler per tournament hour; Krogman 2012), and Georgia (0.29 bass per angler per tournament hour; Peacock 2016). Low tournament bass catch rates throughout the country suggest that length and bag limits would also have limited success at managing the number of bass weighed in during tournaments in many different systems. Throughout recreational fisheries, anglers often do not fill bag limits due to low fish catchability (Cook et al. 2001; Isermann et al. 2007; Isermann and Paukert 2010), potentially in part due to continued angling pressure and learned lure avoidance behaviors of bass (Hessenauer et al. 2016; Wegener et al. 2018). Although the number of bass captured per angler‐hour was low, anglers still captured up to 30% of the Brushy Creek Lake population (Sylvia and Weber 2019; Sylvia et al. 2021b), which is high compared to other studies (Driscoll et al. 2007; Hysmith et al. 2014) due to the large number of tournament angler‐hours on Brushy Creek Lake.

In this study, water temperature and wind speed were more important for describing variability in bass weigh‐in probability than were tournament minimum length and bag limits. Bass catchability can fluctuate throughout a year due to variability in water temperature and other environmental conditions (Hessenauer et al. 2016; Wegener et al. 2018; Sylvia and Weber 2019). We observed a quadratic effect of wind speed on bass weigh‐in probability, with weigh‐in probability being highest at wind speeds around 12 km/h. Wind speed may affect bass movement behaviors and feeding patterns (Warden and Lorio 1975; Lupandin 2005) and lake conditions (Cózar et al. 2005) while also making boat control and angling difficult, thus forcing anglers to alter their techniques and fish in areas that are protected from the wind. Beyond affecting catchability, bass tournament mortality is also generally highest during warm water temperatures (Wilde 1998; Keretz et al. 2018; Sylvia and Weber 2019; Maahs et al. 2021). Therefore, using environmental conditions to regulate tournament events (e.g., no tournaments or catch‐and‐immediate‐release tournaments at water temperatures >26°C) may be more effective than length and bag limits for reducing the number of bass exposed to tournament‐related stress through tournament weigh‐ins.

Beyond environmental conditions, the observed number of bass weighed in at tournaments was positively related to bass weigh‐in probability, suggesting that other angler and tournament characteristics that we were unable to directly quantify were important in describing variability in bass weigh‐in probability. We observed no effect of angler effort on bass weigh‐in probability, suggesting that regulations limiting the number of anglers would not be an effective management technique (e.g., >40% of anglers did not weigh in a bass). Instead, variable angling strategies, techniques, and angler skill may all contribute to variability in bass weigh‐in probability (Meka 2004; Heermann et al. 2013; Sylvia et al. 2020) rather than just angler effort or minimum length and bag limits. Our maximum estimate of bass weigh‐in probability was 0.0342, with many estimates <0.01, indicating that only a small proportion of the bass population in Brushy Creek Lake is weighed in at each tournament.

Although regulations used by the tournaments in this study did not affect bass weigh‐in probability, we estimated that the number of bass weighed in would be reduced by over 30% if bag limits were one or two bass or if minimum length limits were greater than 381 mm. Minimum length and bag limits are generally considered effective management tools to reduce harvest and manipulate population size structure (Noble and Jones 1999; Radomski et al. 2001). In harvest‐oriented fisheries for Black Crappie Pomoxis nigromaculatus, Yellow Perch Perca flavescens, and Walleye Sander vitreus, few anglers fill their bag limit and large reductions in daily bag limits are generally necessary before population benefits are achieved (typically a harvest reduction >25%; Isermann et al. 2007; Mosel et al. 2015; Moreau and Matthias 2018). Although relaxing bag limits resulted in few additional bass being weighed in, removing minimum length limits allowed anglers to weigh in bass that they would have immediately released under the previous 381‐mm minimum length limit. For the 12 tournaments that implemented a minimum length <381 mm, we estimated that the number of bass weighed in would have been reduced by 40% if a 381‐mm minimum length limit had been implemented. Consequently, our results indicate that bag and minimum length limits can reduce the number of bass weighed in at tournaments, but limiting tournament anglers at Brushy Creek Lake to one or two bass larger than 381 mm may not be biologically necessary at this time or supported by tournament anglers.

To provide population‐level benefits in a tournament‐dominated catch‐and‐release fishery, regulations not only need to reduce the number of bass weighed in, but also need to reduce tournament mortality. Our study identified a mismatch between the period of highest delayed mortality and the period when the greatest reduction in number of bass weighed in was estimated to occur under simulated liberalized length and bag limits. Therefore, a bag limit of one or two bass may reduce the number of bass weighed in during months when catch rates are higher but may have little effect on tournament mortality due to cooler water temperatures and lower tournament mortality. For example, a simulated one‐bass bag limit reduced the number of bass weighed in by 66% in June 2018 and by 49% in August 2018, but tournament mortality was greater in August (7%), when temperatures were warmer compared to June (4%). Population‐level effects of tournament mortality on bass populations are unknown, although tournament mortality less than 15% may not affect bass abundance or size structure (Allen et al. 2004). If tournament mortality is additive, then all reductions in tournament mortality would reduce total mortality and benefit bass populations, but if mortality is compensatory, then large reductions in tournament mortality would be necessary before tournament regulations would be beneficial (Allen et al. 1998). Bass populations can exhibit a partial compensatory response to exploitation (Allen et al. 1998). Thus, in populations like that in Brushy Creek Lake, where natural mortality greatly exceeds tournament mortality (natural mortality = 57%; tournament mortality = 6%; Sylvia et al. 2021a), small reductions in tournament mortality may not benefit populations, thus limiting the effectiveness of tournament length and bag limits in these situations. Alternatively, reductions in tournament mortality could benefit other bass populations with lower natural mortality and/or higher tournament mortality.

Biologists throughout Iowa were concerned about how the liberalized tournament regulations enacted in 2018 would affect bass populations. Our results at Brushy Creek Lake suggest that bag limits of three or more bass with length limits of 381 mm or less have little effect on the number of bass weighed in at this system. Consequently, liberalized tournament regulations do not appear to be adversely affecting the Brushy Creek Lake bass population and may provide value through increased tournament angler participation and satisfaction. However, these sorts of regulations that treat subsets of anglers differently can create inequities and conflict among angler groups. Thus, managers should carefully consider the social and biological ramifications of more liberalized tournament regulations before implementation. We intentionally selected Brushy Creek Lake for this study due to the high tournament effort and the high proportion of the bass population captured annually. However, the high natural mortality and high recruitment at Brushy Creek Lake due an abundance of aquatic vegetation and woody habitat likely offset current tournament mortality; other systems with lower natural mortality and/or recruitment may be more affected by tournament mortality (see Kerns et al. 2016; Sass et al. 2018). We encourage additional population‐level assessments of tournament mortality to better understand how it affects populations and the conditions under which those effects are observed.

We found no effect of tournament regulations on the number of bass weighed in, but our results could change in the future through continued improvements in fishing technology that may improve anglers' ability to locate and capture bass (Cooke et al. 2021). The need for more conservative tournament regulations also varies among fisheries. For some populations, 5% of legal‐length bass are weighed in and tournament mortality contributes 1–16% of total annual mortality (Driscoll et al. 2007), while in other instances, up to 43% of bass populations are weighed in annually, resulting in tournament mortality that represents a high proportion of fishing mortality (Hysmith et al. 2014). In Brushy Creek Lake, 27–30% of 381–457‐mm bass and 28–37% of bass larger than 457 mm were weighed in at tournaments annually, resulting in tournament mortality of 7–10% of bass from each size category (Sylvia and Weber 2022). When fishery managers are concerned about tournament mortality, daily bag limits would need to be reduced to one or two bass and/or length limits would need to be increased to over 381 mm to reduce the number of bass that are exposed to tournament stress and associated mortality. Alternatively, improving live‐well and tournament weigh‐in conditions (e.g., oxygenation systems and wet weigh‐ins) could be alternative management actions for reducing tournament mortality (Keretz et al. 2018; Cooke et al. 2020). Identifying new ways to reduce the number of bass that are exposed to tournament‐induced stress may also provide a more viable management method. For example, some professional‐level tournaments have transitioned to a release‐at‐capture format whereby anglers weigh and measure the bass on their boats and then immediately release the bass without exposing them to live‐well confinement and prolonged handling; however, these types of tournaments create additional logistical complexities (Cooke et al. 2020) and may not be supported by anglers. Limiting the number of tournaments could also reduce the number of bass that are weighed in—particularly during periods of high bass catchability—but would limit angling opportunities and likely would be unpopular with tournament anglers. Thus, managing tournaments is a complex process that must consider both biological and social aspects. Our results suggest that bass tournament mortality may be low for some populations and that management actions to reduce tournament mortality may be unnecessary. However, if tournament mortality is a concern, finding a balance between biological benefits and social acceptability is critical when managing tournaments.
